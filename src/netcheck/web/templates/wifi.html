Check your Wi-Fi yourself

No website can see inside your Wi-Fi, but you can, in about 10 minutes, from your router's app or settings page. Work through these in order; the first three matter most.

Getting into your router

  • Most providers have an app (search your provider's name plus "app") that shows your Wi-Fi settings and connected devices.
  • Otherwise, the router's settings page address and admin password are usually printed on a label on the router.
{% endif %}

1. Who's on your Wi-Fi?

Find the list of connected devices (often called "Connected devices", "Attached devices" or "Clients"). Count your own: phones, laptops, TVs, speakers, the doorbell, the thermostat. Names are often unhelpful, like "ESP_3A1F" or a string of numbers.

If something doesn't add up, change your Wi-Fi password. Every device has to reconnect with the new one, so anything that shouldn't be there gets kicked off. Reconnect your own devices one by one; anything you can't account for afterwards wasn't yours.

2. Is your Wi-Fi properly encrypted?

In your Wi-Fi settings, look for "Security", "Encryption" or "Authentication":

  • WPA3 or WPA2 (sometimes "WPA2-PSK" or "WPA2/WPA3"): good.
  • WEP, WPA (without the 2), or Open / None: change it now. These can be broken or need no password at all.

3. Change default passwords

There are two passwords: the Wi-Fi password (to join the network) and the router admin password (to change its settings). If either is still the one printed on the label, change it. Anyone who's seen the label (a visitor, a previous tenant, a repair person) knows it.

A good Wi-Fi password is long rather than complicated: four or five random words ("lamp-river-cobalt-tuesday") beats "P@ssw0rd!".

4. Turn off features you don't use

  • WPS (the push-button/PIN pairing): it's easy to attack on many routers.
  • Remote management ("Remote access", "Web access from WAN"): lets the router's settings page be reached from the internet.
  • UPnP: lets devices open holes in your firewall by themselves. Turn it off unless a game console or app needs it.
  • Port forwarding / DMZ: delete any rule you didn't set up on purpose.

5. Keep the router updated

Look for "Firmware", "Software update" or "Router update" and turn on automatic updates if it's offered. Routers from your internet provider usually update themselves. If your own router hasn't had an update in years, it's time to replace it: old routers are a favourite target of botnets.

6. Put visitors and gadgets on a guest network

Most routers can run a separate "guest" Wi-Fi. Use it for visitors and for smart-home gadgets (cameras, plugs, TVs), which are rarely updated. If one of them is compromised, it can't reach your laptop or phone.

Signs something may be wrong

  • Your router's settings changed without you, or the admin password no longer works.
  • Websites redirect somewhere strange, or you see ads where there shouldn't be any.
  • Your internet is suddenly slow with nothing running, or your provider warns you about suspicious traffic.
  • Devices you don't recognise keep reappearing after a password change.

If you think your network has been hacked

  1. Factory-reset the router (usually a small reset button held for 10 to 30 seconds). Routers from your provider usually set themselves back up; with your own router, you'll need to set it up again.
  2. Immediately set new passwords for both the Wi-Fi and the router admin page.
  3. Update the router's firmware, then work through steps 2 to 4 above.
  4. Update and scan your devices, starting with computers. Change important passwords (email first) from a device you trust.
